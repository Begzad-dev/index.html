01

Approach

Each alert (signal_id) comes with a variable-length history of raw transactions — a median of 461 transactions spanning up to 180 days. Since the target lives at the alert level and the evidence lives at the transaction level, the core of the problem is summarizing many transaction rows into one row per alert, then learning which summary statistics separate escalated alerts from dismissed ones.

We worked in three passes: explore the raw and aggregated data to find where the signal actually lives, engineer features guided by what we found, then fit and validate a model — deliberately choosing model complexity based on cross-validation rather than assuming a more complex model would win.

Dataset overview

Alerts (*_signals.csv)

  • 14,000 train / 6,000 test alerts
  • One row per alert: ID, alert date, and (train only) the escalation label
  • No missing values

Transactions (*_transactions.parquet)

  • ~7.0M train / ~3.0M test rows, relational to alerts
  • Direction (kirim/chiqim), type (card, bank transfer, cash, international)
  • Standardized transaction-size index, timestamped

The escalation signal is not a simple rule like "more cash" or "more international transfers." It has to live in aggregate, alert-level behavior — volume, volatility, and timing patterns across a full history — which is exactly what shaped our feature engineering next.
04

Features → model

Guided by the EDA, every alert's transaction history is collapsed into ~38 features across four groups:

  • Volume & amount — count, mean/std/min/max/median/sum of the size index
  • Category ratios — direction, transaction-type mix, night-time share, weekend share, outlier-amount share
  • Timing — days-before-alert range, gaps between consecutive transactions, active-day density
  • Recency & trend — activity in the last 7/14/30/60 days, and how the recent average amount compares to the alert's own historical average
fig. 6 — top LightGBM feature importances, averaged across 5 folds

Even the strongest individual features correlate weakly with the target (all |r| < 0.07) — consistent with the transaction-level finding above. We fit a LightGBM classifier, and deliberately kept it shallow (num_leaves=3) and heavily regularized: in cross-validation, more complex trees consistently scored lower, a sign they were fitting noise rather than signal. The final submission averages 10 bagged models (different random seeds) trained on the full data.

05

Conclusion

The strongest, most reliable finding from this challenge was where the signal isn't: individual transactions carry almost no information about escalation, so any model working row-by-row on raw transactions would be flying blind. The (real but modest) predictive power comes from summarizing behavior across an alert's full history — particularly amount range, direction ratio, cash usage, and how recent activity trends relative to an alert's own baseline.

Given the weak per-transaction discriminative signal in this synthetic dataset, a 5-fold CV ROC-AUC of ~0.598 from a shallow, regularized model reflects a realistic, non-overfit ceiling on this feature set — larger models did not generalize better.
